Introduction

LTM Energy is already operating at the intersection of engineering, renewable generation, battery storage, energy management, digital monitoring and customer energy services. Through eSUMS and its broader technology ecosystem, the company has already moved beyond basic energy monitoring into more advanced operational intelligence.

This proposal therefore does not begin from the premise that LTM needs to ‘adopt AI’. The opportunity is more specific: to establish an Energy Intelligence & AI Centre of Excellence that helps LTM identify where AI, optimisation, software and engineering intelligence can create measurable operational value, strengthen existing digital capabilities and develop new commercial offerings.

The proposed Centre of Excellence is intended to be small, practical and outcome-driven. It would work with LTM’s existing engineers, digital systems and technology partners rather than attempt to replace them. Its role would be to identify high-value problems, rapidly build and test solutions, establish appropriate governance, and scale only the capabilities that demonstrate real value.

Throughout this proposal, selected concepts are demonstrated using illustrative simulated data. These interactive examples are not representations of LTM’s actual operational data; they are intended to show how the proposed capabilities could work in practice.

1. The Strategic Opportunity

LTM already has many of the building blocks required for a differentiated Energy Intelligence capability: engineering expertise, renewable-energy projects, battery systems, eSUMS, operational data, customer relationships and specialist technology partners.

The strategic opportunity is to connect these capabilities more deliberately. Instead of treating monitoring, forecasting, optimisation, engineering knowledge and AI as separate initiatives, LTM can progressively combine them into a decision layer that links physical conditions to technical consequences, financial impact and recommended action.

“What if every LTM-managed energy asset could tell us not only what is happening, but what matters, what it is costing, what should happen next and whether the intervention worked?”

The proposed Energy Intelligence capability is intended to answer that question. It builds on LTM’s existing engineering and digital foundation and moves beyond isolated alerts or reports toward an operating model that can understand, quantify, optimise, act, verify and learn.

This progression does not imply replacing deterministic engineering systems with AI. Engineering models, verified data and established control systems remain the source of technical truth. AI is used where it adds value in reasoning, orchestration, explanation and interaction.

“Turn LTM’s engineering expertise, operational data, digital platforms and customer footprint into a compounding Energy Intelligence capability.”

2. Energy Intelligence in Practice

Energy-management platforms can generate large volumes of telemetry, alerts, forecasts and analytical information. That information is useful, but the operational challenge is not simply detecting more events. It is deciding which conditions matter most and where intervention will create the greatest value.

A more advanced Energy Intelligence layer can connect a technical condition to its likely energy impact, financial exposure, urgency and recommended next action. This allows engineers and executives to prioritise work based not only on severity, but on consequence.

3. Energy Economics & Optimisation

Operational intelligence helps identify where value is being lost. The next step is to determine what operating decision creates the greatest value under a given set of technical and commercial constraints.

For LTM, this could progressively combine renewable-generation forecasts, customer load, battery state, tariffs, operating limits, resilience requirements and other commercial inputs. The purpose is not simply to predict what will happen, but to determine the best action available.

This is not primarily a generative-AI problem. Forecasting, optimisation, engineering constraints and deterministic economic calculations should provide the technical result. AI can then help orchestrate those tools and explain the recommendation in a form that engineers, operators and executives can use.

Operational intelligence can also support a forward-looking question: given technical and commercial constraints, what operating strategy creates the greatest value?

The strategic opportunity is broader than battery dispatch alone. The same architecture can progressively support decisions involving solar generation, customer demand, tariff structures, load shifting, PPAs, wheeling, resilience and, where commercially relevant, market participation.

The important distinction is that LTM would not rely on a language model to invent an operating strategy. The technical recommendation would come from approved forecasting, optimisation and engineering tools. AI would provide the orchestration and explanation around those tools.

Over time, successful optimisation capabilities could move from internal decision support into customer-facing services or premium eSUMS functionality, creating a potential path toward recurring digital revenue.

4. Building the Capability

LTM does not need to create a large standalone AI department to begin. The proposed Centre of Excellence should start as a small cross-functional capability that brings together executive direction, energy-domain expertise, software and AI engineering, eSUMS, operational teams and specialist technology partners.

Its role is not to own every technology component. Its role is to ensure that LTM is solving the right problems, using appropriate engineering and AI methods, measuring business outcomes and retaining control of the capabilities that could become strategically valuable.

The Centre of Excellence should therefore coordinate six responsibilities: strategy and opportunity selection, AI product development, applied AI engineering, data and knowledge architecture, AI assurance and governance, and commercialisation.

A small operating nucleus

Initially, the Centre of Excellence can operate through a small core team rather than a large new structure. An executive sponsor provides strategic direction; an Applied AI / Energy Intelligence Engineer turns selected opportunities into working systems; LTM energy specialists provide engineering truth and operating constraints; and the eSUMS/digital team ensures alignment with existing platforms and partners. Additional expertise can be brought in when required.

Build, Buy or Partner

A strong Centre of Excellence should not measure success by how much technology LTM builds internally. Commodity capabilities should be purchased or sourced from specialist providers where that is faster, safer or more economical.

The strategic question is where ownership creates an advantage. LTM should seek to retain control over the decision logic, knowledge, workflows and operating intelligence that reflect its engineering experience and customer relationships, while using external technology where the underlying capability is widely available.

Move quickly, but measure rigorously

The Centre of Excellence should also operate at a pace consistent with modern AI-assisted engineering. Discovery, prototyping and user testing do not need to occur as long sequential phases. They can run in parallel, allowing LTM to move from an identified problem to a working proof within days or weeks.

Speed, however, should not replace discipline. Every initiative should still begin with a baseline, define a measurable target and receive a clear Scale / Iterate / Buy / Partner / Stop decision based on evidence.

Successful proofs move into real workflows and production-quality integration. During this period LTM should operationalise the strongest Energy Intelligence capabilities, begin capturing intervention and outcome data, and test whether selected capabilities can strengthen eSUMS, managed services or new recurring digital offerings.

Capabilities that continue to demonstrate value can then expand across additional assets, customers and decision problems. The emphasis shifts from experimentation to scale, adoption, commercialisation and the accumulation of reusable LTM intelligence.

Some strategic advantages require operating history rather than development time. As more projects, interventions and outcomes are captured, LTM can progressively build stronger benchmarks, decision intelligence and cross-site knowledge.

Make every LTM project increase the intelligence of the next one.

The purpose of the Centre of Excellence is therefore not to centralise every AI initiative or build every technology internally. It is to give LTM a repeatable mechanism for selecting high-value problems, combining engineering and digital capabilities, proving value quickly, governing risk appropriately and turning successful intelligence into operational and commercial advantage.

5. Business Value & Proposed Contribution

The value of an Energy Intelligence & AI Centre of Excellence should ultimately be measured in business and operational outcomes, not in the number of models, agents or prototypes deployed.

For LTM, the opportunity spans several forms of value: protecting energy and revenue, improving engineering productivity, strengthening customer outcomes, increasing the value of existing digital services and creating new recurring commercial offerings.

The purpose of the first proofs is therefore not simply to demonstrate that the technology works. It is to determine which capabilities create enough measurable value to justify operationalisation and scale.

Where value can be created

Protect energy and revenue

Earlier identification and prioritisation of underperformance can help reduce avoidable energy loss, shorten intervention time and protect the economic value of managed assets.

Potential measures

  • recovered generation
  • avoided downtime
  • Energy-at-Risk addressed
  • revenue protected
  • intervention time

Improve engineering productivity

Controlled AI and engineering tools can reduce time spent gathering information, investigating recurring problems, retrieving technical knowledge and preparing operational analysis.

Potential measures

  • investigation time
  • engineering hours saved
  • report preparation time
  • information retrieval time
  • cases handled per engineer

Improve customer outcomes

Energy Intelligence can help LTM demonstrate not only that assets are being monitored, but where value is being created, protected or optimised for the customer.

Potential measures

  • performance improvement
  • optimisation value identified
  • response time
  • customer adoption
  • retention

Strengthen existing digital services

Successful intelligence capabilities can increase the value of eSUMS and LTM’s managed energy services by turning operational information into prioritised recommendations, economic insights and decision support.

Potential measures

  • active usage
  • feature adoption
  • service differentiation
  • customer retention
  • managed-service margin

Create recurring digital revenue

Capabilities that demonstrate customer value may eventually become premium eSUMS functionality, Managed Energy Intelligence services, optimisation services or other recurring digital offerings.

Potential measures

  • paying customers
  • recurring revenue
  • revenue per managed site
  • digital-service margin
  • expansion revenue

Measure outcomes, not AI activity

Every material initiative should begin with a baseline and a measurable target. Once tested, it should receive a clear Scale / Iterate / Buy / Partner / Stop decision. This keeps the Centre of Excellence focused on business evidence rather than technological novelty.

  2. Target
  3. Build
  4. Measure
  5. Scale / Iterate / Partner / Stop

An AI initiative should therefore be able to connect to at least one of six outcomes: revenue, cost, risk, productivity, customer value or strategic capability.

Proposed initial contribution

The proposed Centre of Excellence requires a capability that can work across engineering problems, software systems and applied AI. My proposed contribution would sit at that intersection.

My background combines Electrical & Electronic Engineering with software engineering and applied AI development. My experience includes Python-based backend systems, APIs, full-stack applications, AI agents and tool calling, retrieval-grounded workflows, automation and autonomous engineering systems.

My Master’s research involved the design and physical implementation of a voice-enabled autonomous UAV system, including Pixhawk, ArduPilot, MAVLink, Python and local neural speech recognition. An important engineering principle in that work was the separation of probabilistic intelligence from deterministic safety-critical control, a principle directly relevant to industrial and energy AI.

I have also developed applied-AI software in which language models interact with controlled application tools rather than directly inventing system state. This experience aligns with the architecture proposed throughout this document: verified data and engineering systems provide technical truth, while AI provides orchestration, reasoning and interaction.

My strongest contribution would therefore not be to replace LTM’s energy specialists or existing technology partners. It would be to work with them to turn selected high-value problems into functioning AI-enabled systems, establish sound applied-AI engineering practices and help successful proofs progress toward production and commercial use.

Recommended next step

The recommendation is not to approve a large AI transformation programme.

It is to establish a small Energy Intelligence & AI Centre of Excellence, select two or three high-value LTM problems and prove within 30 days whether the proposed approach can create measurable operational or commercial value.

Successful capabilities can then move directly into a focused 3–6 month operationalisation and commercialisation programme. Capabilities that do not demonstrate sufficient value should be changed, sourced from partners or stopped.

LTM already has significant engineering capability, digital-energy infrastructure, customer relationships and operating experience. The opportunity is to make those assets increasingly intelligent, and to ensure that the knowledge generated by one project improves the decisions made on the next.

Make every LTM project increase the intelligence of the next one.
